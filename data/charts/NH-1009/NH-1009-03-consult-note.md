# Allergy and Immunology Consultation — Gideon Ashworth (NH-1009)

## Reason for Consultation

Mr. Ashworth is referred by his Internal Medicine attending physician for evaluation of a reported Myrexatin (tavoxine) allergy. The allergy label has been carried in his chart since 2019. It already influenced antibiotic selection during an inpatient admission for left lower leg cellulitis in 2024, when he was treated with Quinavort (selvoxin) instead. The referring clinician and the patient would like to know whether the label is accurate before any future antibiotic need arises, so that the most appropriate agent can be chosen without an unnecessary restriction. The specific question is whether the 2019 reaction represents a true, clinically significant hypersensitivity to Myrexatin, and whether the label can safely be removed.

## History of Present Illness

Mr. Ashworth is a 58-year-old man with plaque dermatitis of both forearms and a history of resolved left leg cellulitis. In 2019 he developed a painful lower molar dental infection. His dentist prescribed a short oral course of Myrexatin. He took the first dose in the evening and continued as directed for the next two days with no symptoms. He reports he had never taken Myrexatin or any closely related agent before that course, to his knowledge.

On the third day of the course, in the late afternoon, he noticed raised, itchy, red welts on his upper arms and across his trunk. He describes them as "mosquito-bite-like bumps" that came and went over the course of several hours, with individual welts lasting less than a day. He did not notice any swelling of his lips, tongue, eyelids or throat. He had no hoarseness, no difficulty swallowing, no wheezing, no chest tightness, no shortness of breath, no lightheadedness and no fainting. He had no vomiting, abdominal cramping or diarrhea. He had no fever, joint pain or joint swelling at the time. He did not have blistering, peeling skin, sores in the mouth, eye redness or painful skin. He did not seek emergency care.

He telephoned the dental office, which advised him to stop Myrexatin. He took no further doses. He was switched to a different antibiotic to finish treating the dental infection and does not recall which one. He states the welts faded over the following two to three days and did not recur. He had no lasting skin changes. He does not remember having had a concurrent viral illness at the time, although he notes the dental infection itself made him feel generally run down that week.

Since 2019 he has avoided Myrexatin. He has not knowingly been re-exposed to it. During the 2024 cellulitis admission, the inpatient team selected Quinavort because of the listed allergy, and he tolerated that course well without any rash. He has had no other episodes of hives, either with medications or spontaneously, before or after 2019. He has never experienced anaphylaxis.

## Testing Performed Today

Skin testing to tavoxine (the active ingredient of Myrexatin) was performed on the volar forearms per clinic protocol, with a positive control and a negative control diluent prepared by the clinic pharmacy.

- Controls: the positive control produced an appropriate wheal and flare, confirming the skin was reactive; the negative control produced no wheal, confirming no dermatographism interference.
- Skin prick testing to tavoxine at full concentration: **negative** at 15 minutes (no wheal above the negative control).
- Intradermal testing to tavoxine at the standard dilution: **negative** at 15 minutes (no expansion of the injection bleb and no flare).
- The patient tolerated testing without any symptoms. Vital signs remained stable during and after testing.

Interpretation: negative skin prick and intradermal testing to tavoxine. Skin testing has limited ability to exclude delayed reactions on its own, so the clinic standard is to confirm with a supervised graded oral challenge before removing the label.

## Assessment

1. **Reported Myrexatin (tavoxine) allergy, low-risk history.** The 2019 reaction consisted of isolated, transient hives beginning on day 3 of the course, without angioedema, respiratory, cardiovascular or gastrointestinal involvement, without blistering, mucosal involvement or organ involvement, and resolving within days of stopping the drug. A concurrent infection may have contributed. There has been no anaphylaxis and no severe cutaneous reaction. Skin prick and intradermal testing to tavoxine today were negative with valid controls. Taken together, this is a low-risk history, and the reaction is **likely not a true allergy** to Myrexatin. A supervised graded oral challenge is appropriate to confirm tolerance.
2. Plaque dermatitis of both forearms, improving on Dermasolix; not a contraindication to the challenge, as long as the plaques are stable on the day of testing.
3. History of left leg cellulitis, resolved; future skin infections are plausible given occupational exposure, which increases the value of clarifying the allergy label.

## Addendum

Addendum dated 2025-04-02, Allergy and Immunology procedure area. The patient returned today as planned for a supervised graded oral challenge to Myrexatin (tavoxine). Before starting, he confirmed he had taken no oral allergy-relief medication in the preceding 7 days, had no fever or acute illness, and had no active hives; the forearm plaques were stable. Baseline vital signs were blood pressure 124/78 mmHg, heart rate 70, respiratory rate 14, oxygen saturation 98% on room air and temperature 36.6 °C, and the baseline skin and lung examinations were documented. Step one was one tenth of the therapeutic dose by mouth, followed by a 30-minute observation period with vital signs and a skin, mouth and lung check at 15 and 30 minutes. Step two was the remaining nine tenths, completing the full therapeutic dose, followed by a 60-minute observation period with vital signs every 15 minutes and a repeat skin and lung examination at the end of observation. A registered nurse was present throughout, and emergency medications and equipment were at the bedside per the clinic anaphylaxis protocol. The patient was given written instructions to watch for any late rash, itching or swelling and to report by phone, and a 24-hour follow-up phone call was arranged.

Outcome: the patient tolerated the full therapeutic dose of the challenged agent. There was no hives, itching, flushing, swelling, wheezing, throat symptoms, gastrointestinal symptoms or change in vital signs at any point during the 60-minute observation period, and the final examination was normal. At the 24-hour phone follow-up he reported no rash, itching, swelling or other symptoms. Conclusion: the reported allergy is not confirmed, and the patient is not allergic to the challenged agent. The allergy label has been removed from the chart today, and the problem list and medication list allergy sections have been updated. The patient may receive the challenged agent in the future when clinically indicated. The result was discussed with the patient and communicated to the referring Internal Medicine clinician.
